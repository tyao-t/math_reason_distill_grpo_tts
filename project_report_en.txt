================================================================================
Project Report: Distillation (SFT) + GRPO + Test-Time Scaling Pipeline for
Small-Model Math Reasoning
================================================================================

Goals and Motivation
----------------

Starting from a small base model (Qwen3-0.6B-Base), improve its math reasoning
ability in three stages:

  (1) Distillation (SFT): Generate step-by-step proof-style answers from a
      stronger teacher model, filter them, then run SFT with hard labels.
      In this project "distillation" and "SFT" are actually the same thing —
      we did not use soft logit distillation (the reasons are described
      below); instead we treat the teacher's outputs as ground truth and let
      the small model do supervised fine-tuning directly. So the
      distillation stage and the SFT stage are described jointly below.

  (2) GRPO: On math problems with verifiable answers, use GRPO to further
      strengthen the checkpoint produced by Distillation (SFT).

  (3) Test-time scaling / Inference-time scaling: At evaluation time we do
      not look only at single-sample accuracy, but instead use a set of
      metrics — greedy / avg@1 / cons@N / wcons@N / pass@N — combined
      with majority vote, logprob-weighted voting, and tie breaking to
      squeeze out extra performance. The student's chain of thought is
      not triggered by prompts like "let's think step by step" nor wrapped
      in <think> tags; the step-by-step format is baked into the weights
      during Distillation (SFT). Logprob-triggered self-refinement and
      adaptive-N early stopping are natural next-step extensions.

RL methods such as GRPO / DAPO have a long engineering pipeline, depend on
verifiable rewards, and are themselves already relatively mature methods, so
their marginal returns are limited. This project therefore puts its main
effort on distillation and test-time scaling, taking the final model
performance as the yardstick. To simplify the training pipeline, the GRPO
stage does not introduce additional pretraining / replay tasks for
mitigating catastrophic forgetting; this trade-off is consistent with the
removal of the KL-divergence penalty term described below.

The report roughly follows the order in which the scripts in the repository
are run.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Distillation (SFT): Data Generation
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The goal of the first stage is to construct a high-quality (problem, answer)
corpus, in which the answer is generated by a teacher model rather than
written by humans.
- Related script: gen_distill_answers.py (batch-samples answers from the
  teacher)
- Output: qwen3-235b-a22b-distillation-answer-pairs.jsonl (the unfiltered
  raw distillation outputs; correctness / perplexity filtering happens in
  the next section)

  # With the teacher (Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct-2507, TP or EP=8) already
  # served, call the teacher to batch-generate distillation answers
  python gen_distill_answers.py \
    --base-url http://localhost:8000/v1 \
    --model qwen3 \
    --input math_full_minus_math500.json \
    --output distillation_results_new.jsonl \
    --num-questions 12500 \
    --num-samples 1 \
    --concurrency 64 \
    --max-tokens 3072

The overall design has three interrelated threads: the choice of teacher,
the design of the prompt, and the control of trace length. The shared
purpose of all three is to let the 0.6B student really learn the proofs
themselves within a limited token budget.

- Teacher model selection
  We use the instruct (non-thinking) tier of the Qwen3-235B-A22B-2507
  family, deliberately not Qwen3-235B-A22B-Thinking-2507 nor any 2507
  thinking-series checkpoint. The Thinking series trades extremely long
  chain-of-thought for reasoning ability, and its outputs are filled with
  self-correcting, backtracking, repeatedly-agonizing phrases such as
  "wait...", "reconsider...", "actually, let me redo step 2...". This is
  not a problem for large-model students, but for a 0.6B student the
  signal-to-noise ratio of an 8k+ token long monologue is too low: the
  truly useful proof skeleton gets drowned out by verbose filler and
  becomes noise instead. Even the smaller thinking variants frequently
  open with filler like "This is a difficult or complex problem.", which
  brings no benefit to distillation. We therefore later use prompts to
  constrain the model's output. We also compared the contemporaneously
  updated Qwen3.6 series: although more capable, its tokenizer and
  prompting / pretraining style are further from this project's target
  preferences, and its architecture introduces large changes such as GDN /
  LA, so its transferability to the 0.6B student is actually worse than
  Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct-2507.

- Prompt design
  The teacher's prompt is specifically tuned to force it to output strict,
  concise, step-by-step proofs, organized in the form
  "Step 1 / Step 2 / Step 3 / ... / Final answer", and explicitly forbids
  free-form inner-monologue, restarts, or any "wait, let me reconsider"
  filler. The intuition: the shorter and more clearly structured the
  trace, the better the chance that a small model absorbs the logical
  structure of the proof, instead of memorizing the surface linguistic
  habits of the teacher.

- Length budget & handling of overlong samples
  The initial distillation data used 2048 tokens as the sequence length
  cap; this was later relaxed to 3072–4096, because some problems really
  need more space to be expressed in full; but it is not relaxed further.
  Samples exceeding this cap are not truncated and then trained on; they
  are dropped entirely. The reasons are at two levels:
    * Direct level: these samples are simply too long, and the 0.6B small
      model can hardly really learn them. Keeping them in the training
      set mostly introduces noise that disturbs overall convergence,
      rather than yielding gains.
    * Indirect level: not dropping them means truncating, and a sample
      cut off in the middle of a proof gives the student the worst
      possible training signal — it teaches the student to "start a
      proof but never end it", not even producing a \boxed{}, which is
      worse than not training on this problem at all.
  So a rough rule is: if even an instruct teacher at the A22B scale
  cannot do the problem within 3072–4096 tokens, treat the problem as
  "out of scope of this pipeline" and drop it, rather than letting the
  trace grow without limit. This rule in turn constrains the previous
  two — choosing the instruct teacher rather than thinking, and forcing
  the prompt to be concise, are essentially both meant to keep more
  problems within this length budget.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. Filtering of Distillation Data
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The teacher's raw outputs contain noise and require two kinds of judgment
and filtering.

2a. Correctness filtering (LLM-as-a-judge)
--------------------------------

- Related script: run_math_judge_vllm.py (starts a vLLM judge service and
  in batches labels each distillation sample as correct / incorrect)
- Input: qwen3-235b-a22b-distillation-answer-pairs.jsonl
- Outputs:
    * qwen3-235b-a22b-distillation-answer-pairs.judged.jsonl
      (all samples + a _judge_verdict field on each sample)
    * qwen3-235b-a22b-distillation-answer-pairs.judged.correct.jsonl
      (the subset keeping only _judge_verdict == "correct", used as the
      main pool for Distillation (SFT))

  # With a mid-to-large FP8 model already served by vLLM as the judge
  # (served-model-name being qwen3-fp8), call run_math_judge_vllm.py
  # to label each sample
  uv run run_math_judge_vllm.py \
    --input_json distillation_results_new.jsonl \
    --solutions_json math_full_minus_math500.json \
    --output_json distillation_results_new_judged.jsonl \
    --base_url http://localhost:8000/v1 \
    --model qwen3-fp8 \
    --max_tokens 2048 \
    --concurrency 64

- Judging is easier than solving. For a problem the judge model itself
  cannot reliably solve, it can still reliably tell whether "this candidate
  proof's final answer is correct". This asymmetry is the fundamental
  reason LLM-as-a-judge is cost-effective.

- Exploiting this asymmetry, the judge can be made to run very fast. Two
  reasonable routes:
    * Use a smaller judge model, e.g. at the Qwen3.6-27B scale, paired with
      FLA / GDN serving. Cheap, but in this project the prompts are
      relatively long (the problem itself + a multi-step proof trace), and
      a too-small judge tends to drift, producing misjudgments.
    * Use a slightly larger judge model + more efficient serving:
      vLLM + TP / EP, with weights in FP8. FP8 fully utilizes tensor
      cores, roughly doubling throughput compared to BF16, while the
      quality loss for tasks like judging is negligible; meanwhile the
      traditional FlashAttention kernels are also more mature. We
      ultimately adopt the FP8 mid-to-large model + vLLM scheme, with the
      best quality / cost ratio.

- The "judged correct" subset (qwen3-235b-a22b-distillation-answer-
  pairs.judged.correct.jsonl) is used as the main pool for Distillation
  (SFT). We also tried keeping a small number of judged-incorrect
  samples: the rationale is that some "wrong" cases are actually the
  judge itself making a misjudgment, and additionally a small number of
  near-correct samples can act as a kind of regularization. But the
  default configuration still drops the misjudged ones.


2b. Perplexity filtering
-------------------

- Related script: compute_perplexity_vllm.py (runs a forward pass with the
  student base model, computes the average token logprob for each
  (problem, answer), then exp's it to get PPL; also includes
  deduplication logic)
- Input: the .judged.correct.jsonl from the previous step (also includes
  intermediate products that have already been deduplicated, such as
  distillation_results_new_judged_correct_dedup.jsonl)
- Output: distillation_results_new_judged_correct_dedup.ppl_filtered.jsonl
  (the Distillation (SFT) training set after cutting off the very-high-PPL
  tier)

  # With the student base model already served by vLLM (port 8000), run
  # compute_perplexity_vllm.py to compute the average logprob → PPL of
  # each sample
  uv run compute_perplexity_vllm.py \
    --data_path distillation_results_new_judged_correct_dedup.jsonl \
    --model /opt/tiger/tianhao.yao/models/qwen3-0.6b-base \
    --host localhost --port 8000 \
    --concurrency 128 \
    --max_seq_len 6143 \
    --out_file perplexity_results.json

The starting point is the common difficulty-filtering idea — "things that
are too hard cannot be learned, things that are too simple are not
meaningful to learn either": samples with too-high PPL are basically beyond
the syllabus of a 0.6B small model, and forcing it to learn them yields
mostly rote memorization rather than generalization; samples with too-low
PPL are information-thin, and even after learning them the gain in model
capability is limited.

But this filtering tool has obvious limitations, so it must be used with
care:

- During the distillation stage, the student needs to learn two things at
  once: (a) the difficulty and content of the math proof itself; (b) the
  teacher's format and organization. Perplexity mixes these two together. A
  short, simple proof, if its format is unfamiliar to the student, can have
  higher PPL than a long, hard proof in a familiar format — exactly the
  opposite of the direction we want to filter on.

- In the actual computation, we average per-token logprob and then exp.
  On long answers, this average gets diluted by a large number of "glue
  tokens" (spaces, $ $, "Step", "Therefore", newlines, etc.). The result
  is that the signal from the few truly difficult tokens is washed out:
  there are both false negatives (truly hard problems whose scores do not
  look high) and false positives (some simple or moderately hard problems
  that get classified into the "high perplexity" range because of some
  unusual phrasing).

- Therefore PPL filtering is positioned as a coarse pre-screen, not a
  final judgment. In actual use, we pair it with manual spot-checks of
  samples near the threshold to make sure the cut line has not gone awry.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. Distillation (SFT) Stage
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

train_qwen3_0.6b_sft.py performs supervised fine-tuning of Qwen3-0.6B on the
filtered distillation set. The output checkpoints are placed under models/,
with names containing the data slice and the training recipe (e.g.
qwen3-0.6b-sft-distill-a22b-allcorrect-6144-mrbl-bs8). This Distillation
(SFT) checkpoint is the starting point for the GRPO stage that follows.

  # Main SFT: starting from qwen3-0.6b-base, train 3 epochs on the
  # filtered + deduplicated distillation set
  uv run train_qwen3_0.6b_sft.py \
    --data_path ./distillation_results_new_judged_correct_dedup.jsonl \
    --model_name_or_path /opt/tiger/tianhao.yao/models/qwen3-0.6b-base \
    --output_dir /opt/tiger/tianhao.yao/models/qwen3-0.6b-sft-distill-a22b-allcorrect-2790-mrbl-bs8-new-dedup-nfa \
    --epochs 3 \
    --per_device_train_batch_size 8 \
    --gradient_accumulation_steps 1 \
    --lr 1.5e-5 \
    --warmup_ratio 0.03 \
    --max_grad_norm 1.0 \
    --max_seq_len 2790 \
    --seed 442 \
    --attn_implementation sdpa \
    --precision bf16-mixed \
    --no_compile_model

A few engineering considerations:

- The truncation length matches the distillation length budget
  (3072–4096). This is exactly why the previous stage went to such effort
  to compress the teacher trace — the Distillation (SFT) loss is computed
  on (potentially truncated) tokens, and once a long trace is cut off in
  the middle of a proof, it amounts to teaching the student to "stop
  halfway through writing", which is the worst possible training signal —
  it cannot even produce a \boxed{}.

- The chain of thought the student learns looks just like the teacher's
  output: a compact "Step 1 / Step 2 / ... / Final answer" with almost no
  filler. This is precisely the entire purpose of the prompt engineering
  and teacher selection in the first stage.

- Why not soft distillation: another common practice is to have the
  student match the teacher's logits distribution (offline soft
  distillation that caches logits, or online soft distillation that lets
  the student sample itself and the teacher score along the trajectory).
  This scheme has advantages on open-ended generation tasks, but for math
  problems with "unique answers", expert experience and our ablations both
  point to the same conclusion: directly learning the teacher's hard
  labels is enough; matching the entire distribution brings more noise
  than it offsets, and cannot make up for the additional infrastructure
  cost (caching logits, long-term serving of the teacher, etc.).
  Therefore the "distillation" in this pipeline is, in implementation,
  equivalent to a regular Distillation (SFT).


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. GRPO Stage
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

After Distillation (SFT) is finished, we run GRPO training (grpo.py,
train_grpo_qwen3_math.py, grpo_reward.py); the training data are math
problems with verifiable string-equivalence rewards (a subset of
DAPO-Math-17k, generated by prepare_dapo_300.py /
extend_dapo_to_600.py; the training files are grpo_train_dapo300.json,
grpo_train_dapo600.json, grpo_train_dapo1200.json respectively).

Points to note:

- GRPO is significantly slower than Distillation (SFT). At every GRPO
  step, in addition to the gradient update, we also have to sample N
  candidates (rollouts) from the current policy and compute rewards; the
  per-step wall-clock is generally several times that of Distillation
  (SFT). We use vLLM as the rollout backend (serve_vllm_grpo.py,
  vllm_client.py) to bring this overhead down.

    # Rollout backend: serve the current policy for the GRPO training
    # loop to sample from
    uv run serve_vllm_grpo.py \
      --model models/qwen3-0.6b-sft-distill-a22b-allcorrect-6144-targeted96-lr1e-7-seed442-2 \
      --port 8000 --max-model-len 8192 \
      --gpu-memory-utilization 0.85 --dtype bfloat16

- Reward design: GRPO's training signal comes entirely from the reward
  function, so the definition of the reward is more critical than the
  training loop itself. This project mainly takes the "verifiable reward
  (RLVR)" route, while also evaluating several options (grpo_reward.py is
  the main implementation, the rest are partly experimented with and
  partly only reserved as interfaces):

    1. Verifiable string/numeric equivalence (main)
       Extract the final answer from \boxed{...}, and compare with the
       ground truth using normalized string equivalence. Cheap,
       deterministic, hallucination-free, the default choice for math
       RLVR. The downside is that for answers that are "semantically
       equivalent but in different forms" (e.g. 1/2 vs 0.5,
       \frac{a+b}{2} vs \frac{b+a}{2}), it gives a false 0; effort needs
       to go into the normalization function.

    2. Math-equivalence verification (sympy / math-verify)
       On top of (1), add a symbolic check: pass both the candidate
       answer and the ground truth through sympy simplification, then
       check equivalence. Can correctly handle cases like algebraic
       identities, trigonometric identities, unit conversions, etc.
       Stricter than pure string but much cheaper than an LLM judge. The
       cost is that sympy occasionally times out or hangs on pathological
       expressions, so timeout protection is needed.

    3. LLM-as-a-judge as reward (vLLM serve)
       Completely isomorphic to the "judging the distillation data
       correctness" in Stage 2: spin up a vLLM service (FP8 mid-to-large
       model + TP/EP), let it score each rollout 0/1 or with a finer
       score, then feed the score back to GRPO. The advantage is that it
       can handle dimensions like the reasonableness of the proof,
       units, phrasing, etc. that string/sympy cannot capture; the
       disadvantages are (a) the judge itself is noisy and introduces
       the risk of reward hacking (the policy may learn to "cheat the
       judge"), (b) each rollout incurs an additional LLM call,
       significantly raising wall-clock, (c) on the training side it is
       best to also apply truncated-importance-sampling-style
       stabilization to the judge. This project already has the
       infrastructure to serve an FP8 judge (run_math_judge_vllm.py),
       and connecting it to the reward hook of GRPO is engineering-ready;
       this is listed as a next-step extension direction.

    4. Format reward
       Give a small additional reward for output format: whether
       \boxed{} is provided, whether it is organized as
       Step 1 / Step 2, whether it contains "<think>" tags, etc. In the
       setup of this project, Distillation (SFT) has already pinned the
       format very tightly, so the marginal benefit of format reward is
       small, and it is not enabled; but if running RLVR directly from
       the base model, this term would become very critical.

    5. Length penalty
       Apply a linear or soft penalty on overlong traces, to prevent the
       policy from boosting accuracy by "stretching the chain
       indefinitely" (a common degeneration mode of RLVR). Can be used
       in conjunction with truncation.

    6. Process / step-level reward (PRM)
       Instead of evaluating only the final answer, score step by step
       along the reasoning. Requires training or obtaining a PRM model
       (process reward model); the most expensive but theoretically the
       densest signal. Under small models + math scenarios, PRM
       stability is still evolving, so this pipeline does not introduce
       it for now; listed as future work.

    7. Multi-reward weighted / normalized aggregation
       The above several rewards can be enabled simultaneously and
       weighted-summed. If used, it is recommended to normalize each
       reward within the group separately before aggregating (see the
       GDPO idea mentioned in paragraph (e) below), to prevent the
       magnitude of one term from swallowing the others.

  The main pipeline currently uses the combination of (1) + (2) (string
  equivalence as primary, sympy as backup); (3), the LLM-judge reward,
  is engineering-ready and is the next-step extension direction.

- For math scenarios with "verifiable scalar rewards", the standard GRPO
  recipe can be simplified considerably. Since GRPO was proposed in 2024
  (DeepSeekMath) and rose to fame in 2025 (DeepSeek-R1), the literature
  has accumulated a long list of improvements. This project considers a
  few of the most prominent of these, some actually applied, others tried
  only in ablation:

    (a) No KL loss (DAPO, Dr. GRPO, DeepSeek V3.2)
        The KL-to-reference term yields very little for math tasks with
        "uniquely verifiable answers". Its original intent is to prevent
        reward hacking and style collapse, but in this project the
        reward is numerical correctness, and the policy has a strong
        Distillation (SFT) prior, so drift will not become pathological.
        Setting the KL coefficient directly to 0 is what lets the model
        actually "move". DeepSeek V3.2 further suggests adjusting KL
        strength by domain — 0 for math, a small positive value for
        general dialogue. Coming from the same line of reasoning: this
        project likewise does not add pretrain / replay tasks for
        mitigating catastrophic forgetting in the GRPO stage; the
        premise in both cases is "the training domain is restricted to
        math, and the SFT prior is already strong enough"; both need to
        be re-evaluated when generalized to general multi-task
        settings.

    (b) Clip-higher (DAPO)
        Asymmetric PPO clipping: the clipping upper bound on the
        positive-advantage side is more lenient than on the
        negative-advantage side (e.g. ε_high = 0.28, ε_low = 0.2).
        Lets the policy more decisively absorb "the right way to
        solve", avoiding repeated oscillation around some point.

    (c) Token-level loss (DAPO)
        Aggregate the loss along the token dimension instead of
        averaging along the sequence dimension, to avoid short
        sequences being underestimated and long sequences being
        flattened. Especially important for long chain-of-thought.

    (d) Zero-gradient filtering + Active sampling (DAPO)
        For problems where all N rollouts in the group are right or all
        wrong, the advantage is all 0 with no contribution to the
        gradient, but they still occupy forward, KV cache, and memory.
        Skip such batches directly to save compute; further, when a
        zero-gradient prompt is encountered, resample the next one,
        until we get one that has gradient signal before doing the
        update, so that every step is really "worth its money".

    (e) Truncated importance sampling (Yao et al., 2025)
        Truncate the importance ratio, to mitigate the variance
        explosion caused by inconsistency between the rollout policy
        and the train policy. Especially relevant in scenarios with
        vLLM rollout + a different precision/implementation training
        backend — the logprobs on the two sides will not be exactly
        consistent to begin with.

  Concretely landed in this pipeline, we explicitly enable (a), (b), as
  well as DAPO-style (c) and (d); (e) and more aggressive changes
  (sequence-level IS, weight clipping, multi-reward normalization) 
  did not enter the main pipeline and are kept for future re-evaluation 
  when bottlenecks are encountered.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. Test-Time Scaling / Evaluation
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The final evaluation is done on MATH-500 (math500_test.json); the
relevant scripts are evaluate_math500_vllm.py and
evaluate_math500_vllm_tts.py with test-time scaling, and the result files
are math500*.json. This project deliberately does not report only one
number, but a set of metrics, because each measures a different aspect:

  # With the SFT checkpoint to be evaluated already served by vLLM
  # (served-model-name being qwen3-0.6b-sft), run the MATH-500
  # evaluation
  uv run evaluate_math500_vllm.py \
    --port 8000 \
    --input_path math500.json \
    --model qwen3-0.6b-sft \
    --mode completions \
    --dataset_size 500 \
    --max_new_tokens 4096 \
    --concurrency 256 \
    --out_file math500_qwen3-0.6b-sft_vllm.json

  # Output example:
  #   MATH-500 (vLLM): 500/500 | ETA: 00s
  #   Accuracy: 46.40% (232/500)  |  wall time: 26.0s
  #   Wrote per-problem results to math500_qwen3-0.6b-sft_vllm.json


- Greedy accuracy: single sampling at temperature=0. The cheapest and
  most reproducible "is the answer the model itself most believes in
  correct" metric. Sensitive to single-token deviations.

- avg@1: at non-zero temperature, the average accuracy over a single
  independent sample. With good calibration it is generally slightly
  below greedy; if the greedy mode happens to be a "confidently wrong"
  answer while the surrounding sample space contains quite a few correct
  answers, then avg@1 will instead be higher than greedy.

- pass@N: the problem counts as solved if any of the N independent
  samples is correct. The upper bound that test-time search can reach.
  Used as a "potential" metric — if pass@N is much greater than avg@1,
  the model knows how to solve some samples, and the bottleneck is in
  the selector rather than capability.

- cons@N (majority vote / self-consistency): take the most frequent
  final answer after N samples. The cheapest test-time scaler, almost
  always better than avg@1 on math tasks.

- wcons@N (confidence-weighted consistency): on top of cons@N, weight
  by per-sample confidence, e.g. the average logprob over the final
  answer span, or the judge's score. With reasonable model
  calibration it is generally better than pure cons@N; with poor
  calibration it degrades but not by much.

Examining greedy / avg@1 / cons@N / wcons@N / pass@N jointly lets one
distinguish different failure modes:
    - greedy low, pass@N high            → sampling / selection problem
                                            (capability is enough but
                                            the selector is not)
    - greedy ≈ pass@N, both low          → capability bottleneck, need
                                            stronger Distillation
                                            (SFT) / GRPO
    - cons@N much higher than avg@1      → policy is correct on average,
                                            but single-sample noise is
                                            large
    - wcons@N much higher than cons@N    → confidence signal is
                                            effective, worth turning
                                            into a formal verifier

Test-time scaling methods currently enabled in this project:

  - Majority vote / self-consistency (cons@N): take the most frequent
    final answer across N independent samples. The cheapest TTS method
    and almost always better than avg@1.
  - Weighted majority vote (wcons@N): the same vote weighted by the
    average logprob over each sample's final-answer span — equivalent
    to self-consistency with a built-in confidence signal.
  - Tie breaking: when votes tie, break the tie by average (or max)
    logprob, preferring the branch the model is more confident in,
    instead of falling back to a random / first-seen pick.
  - Distilled-in chain of thought (not prompt-triggered): the student's
    CoT is not triggered by prompts like "let's think step by step" and
    is not wrapped in <think> tags. The
    "Step 1 / Step 2 / ... / Final answer" format is baked into the
    weights during Distillation (SFT). Reasoning therefore does not
    degrade under system-prompt rewrites or chat-template changes, and
    all TTS strategies above assume "the model reasons by default".

Next-step directions, infrastructure-ready but not yet in pipeline:

  - Self-reflection / self-correction / self-refinement: have the model
    critique and revise its own answer (Self-Refine / Reflexion style),
    triggered by logprob + heuristics — run a second pass only when the
    final-answer span logprob is below a threshold, or when cons@N
    still shows disagreement; after refining, accept once the logprob
    improvement crosses a target, and stop iterating once the
    improvement plateaus (or falls below threshold), so already
    "confident and consistent" samples do not burn extra compute.
  - Logprob-based early exit / adaptive N: while sampling, monitor
    accumulated confidence online (top-answer vote count × average
    logprob); stop as soon as it crosses a threshold, approaching the
    accuracy of fixed N with far fewer samples.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
7. Lessons Learned
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. For a 0.6B student model, the teacher's "style" matters as much as its
   "strength". A weaker but more concise teacher is often a better
   distillation source than a stronger but more verbose one.

2. Perplexity-based data filtering is useful but very crude: the average
   logprob of long answers is easily diluted by simple tokens, and false
   positives are not few. Use it as a hint, in conjunction with the
   judge's correctness filtering and manual spot-checks near the
   threshold; do not use it as the final criterion.

3. LLM-as-a-judge is the link in the data pipeline most worth investing
   FP8 / TP / EP engineering effort in: judging is easier than solving,
   so a "fast large-model judge" is almost always better than a "slow
   small-model judge".

4. Running GRPO on verifiable math rewards, turning off KL + clip-higher
   are two adjustments that are clearly effective. The KL term is mainly
   meant for open-ended tasks.

5. For math distillation, hard-label Distillation (SFT) is more
   cost-effective than soft logit distillation — the key signal is in
   "which token to choose", not in the entire distribution. This is
   also why "distillation" and "SFT" are actually the same thing in this
   project.

6. Evaluation must report a set of metrics
   (greedy / avg@1 / cons@N / wcons@N / pass@N); a single number masks
   whether the bottleneck is capability, sampling, or selection. The TTS
   actually enabled here is the trio of majority vote +
   logprob-weighted vote + tie breaking; the chain of thought is baked
   into the weights by Distillation (SFT) rather than triggered by a
   prompt, so reasoning behavior is robust to system-prompt or
   chat-template changes. Logprob-triggered self-refinement and
   adaptive-N early stopping are the natural next steps — both spend
   extra compute only on uncertain or inconsistent samples, leaving
   already "confident and consistent" ones untouched.
